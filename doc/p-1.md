# Pollard's P−1 method

## The result

**Theorem 1 (Pollard's principle).** Let $N \ge 2$, let $q$ be a prime factor of $N$, let $a$ be an integer with $q \nmid a$, and let $E \ge 1$ be an integer with $q - 1 \mid E$. Then $q$ divides $\gcd(a^E - 1, N)$.

It suffices that the multiplicative order of $a$ modulo $q$ divide $E$; $q - 1 \mid E$ guarantees it for every such $a$. The method finds $q$ when $q - 1$ is smooth, a product of small primes, by taking $E$ to be the product of all small prime powers. The method is Pollard's [Pollard1974](references.md#pollard1974): [MontgomerySilverman1990, the abstract](references.md#montgomerysilverman1990) dates the P−1 algorithm to that paper, and [CrandallPomerance2005, § 5.4](references.md#crandallpomerance2005) presents it.

**Theorem 2 (P−1 on $M_p$).** Let $p$ be an odd prime, let $q$ be a prime factor of $M_p$, and write $q = 2kp + 1$ ([the note on the form of factors](factors-and-trial-factoring.md)). Let $B_1 \le B_2$ be bounds, and for each prime $r \le B_1$ let $e_r$ be the largest exponent with $r^{e_r} \le B_1$. Put

$$E_1 = 2p \prod_{r \le B_1} r^{e_r}, \qquad x = 3^{E_1} \bmod M_p.$$

- **Stage 1.** If $k$ divides $\prod_{r \le B_1} r^{e_r}$, that is, every prime power dividing $k$ is at most $B_1$, then $q$ divides $g_1 = \gcd(x - 1, M_p)$.
- **Stage 2.** If $k = k_1 r$ with $k_1$ as in stage 1 and $r$ a single prime with $B_1 < r \le B_2$, then $q$ divides

$$g_2 = \gcd\Bigl(\prod_{B_1 < r \le B_2} (x^r - 1) \bmod M_p, \; M_p\Bigr).$$

A gcd strictly between 1 and $M_p$ is a factor, prime or not. A gcd equal to $M_p$ means every prime factor was caught at once, and it is no result: the run must be repeated with other bounds to separate them.

**Why the form of factors helps.** In general, P−1 finds $q$ only when the whole of $q - 1$ is smooth. For $M_p$, $q - 1 = 2kp$ always holds the prime $p$, far beyond any practical $B_1$ for the exponents worth testing, so plain P−1 would almost never succeed. Putting $2p$ into $E_1$ removes that factor for free, since the theorem on the form of factors says it is always there, and only $k = (q - 1)/(2p)$ must be smooth. That is a number $2p$ times smaller than $q - 1$, and smaller numbers are smooth more often ([CrandallPomerance2005, § 1.4.5](references.md#crandallpomerance2005)). GIMPS describes the same adaptation: stage 1 finds $q$ when $k$ is $B_1$-smooth, and stage 2 when $k$ has one more factor between $B_1$ and $B_2$ [GIMPSMath](references.md#gimpsmath).

**The stages as computed.**

```text
stage 1:
    E ← 2p · (the product of r^e over the primes r ≤ B1, each r^e the largest power of r not above B1)
    x ← 3 raised to E, modulo M_p, by repeated squaring
    g1 ← gcd(x − 1, M_p)
stage 2:
    y ← 1
    for each prime r with B1 < r ≤ B2:
        y ← y · (x raised to r, minus 1) mod M_p
    g2 ← gcd(y, M_p)
```

Base 3 is safe: $3 \nmid M_p$, since $M_p \equiv 1 \pmod 3$ for odd $p$.

**The cost.** Stage 1 is about $\log_2 E_1$ squarings modulo $M_p$. Since $\ln E_1 - \ln 2p = \sum_{r \le B_1} e_r \ln r$ is Chebyshev's function $\psi(B_1)$, which the prime number theorem makes asymptotic to $B_1$ ([CrandallPomerance2005, § 1.1.5](references.md#crandallpomerance2005)), that is about $B_1 / \ln 2 + \log_2 2p$ squarings. Stage 2 as written steps from one prime $r$ to the next, $x^{r'} = x^r \cdot x^{r' - r}$, with the few powers $x^d$ for the even gaps $d$ kept in a table, so it costs about two multiplications for each prime in $(B_1, B_2]$. Each stage ends in one gcd of $p$-bit numbers, quadratic by the schoolbook method and subquadratic by Schönhage's [Moller2008](references.md#moller2008).

**Faster stage 2s.** Montgomery speeds the second phase with two tables, looking for a match between them ([Montgomery1987, the abstract](references.md#montgomery1987)). Pollard's paper stated the algorithm in theoretical terms that use fast Fourier transform techniques, and Montgomery and Silverman show how that extension works in practice ([MontgomerySilverman1990, the abstract](references.md#montgomerysilverman1990)). Montgomery and Kruppa give a space-efficient form of stage 2 by convolutions ([MontgomeryKruppa2008, the abstract](references.md#montgomerykruppa2008)).

## Proof

**Theorem 1.** By Fermat's little theorem, $a^{q-1} \equiv 1 \pmod q$, since $q \nmid a$. Write $E = m (q - 1)$; then $a^E = (a^{q-1})^m \equiv 1 \pmod q$, so $q \mid a^E - 1$. Also $q \mid N$, so $q$ divides their gcd. The same argument works with the order of $a$ in place of $q - 1$. $\square$

**Base 3.** $2^p \equiv (-1)^p = -1 \pmod 3$ for odd $p$, so $M_p \equiv -2 \equiv 1 \pmod 3$, and the prime factor $q$ of $M_p$ is not 3. So $q \nmid 3$, and Theorem 1 applies with $a = 3$.

**Stage 1.** Let $P = \prod_{r \le B_1} r^{e_r}$, so $E_1 = 2pP$. If $k \mid P$, then $q - 1 = 2pk$ divides $2pP = E_1$, and Theorem 1 with $N = M_p$ gives $q \mid \gcd(3^{E_1} - 1, M_p)$. Reducing $3^{E_1}$ modulo $M_p$ changes it by a multiple of $M_p$, hence of $q$, so $q \mid \gcd(x - 1, M_p) = g_1$. A prime power $r^f$ divides $P$ exactly when $r \le B_1$ and $f \le e_r$, that is, $r^f \le B_1$; so $k \mid P$ exactly when every prime power dividing $k$ is at most $B_1$.

**Stage 2.** Let $k = k_1 r$ with $k_1 \mid P$ and $r$ prime, $B_1 < r \le B_2$. Then $q - 1 = 2pk_1 r$ divides $E_1 r$, so $x^r \equiv 3^{E_1 r} \equiv 1 \pmod q$, and $q$ divides the factor $x^r - 1$ of the product. Reducing the product modulo $M_p$ keeps it divisible by $q$, and $q \mid M_p$, so $q \mid g_2$. $\square$

**The example.** The check spans on $k$ show that $k = k_1 r$, where every prime power dividing $k_1$ is at most $B_1$ and $r$ is a prime with $B_1 < r \le B_2$. So the stage 2 statement applies, and Cole's factor divides $g_2$. Stage 1 is not assured, since $r$ does not divide $E_1$. The computed gcds agree, and show that stage 1 found nothing. They are computed in the cycle's record by an independent implementation of exactly the stages above, base 3, with $E_1$ as defined.

## What mps uses

Today P−1 is a stub that decides nothing. `test_p_minus_one` in `src/math.cpp` logs that it is running and returns a no-verdict result, `Result::no_verdict`, saying P−1 is not implemented yet. `-t p1` runs it alone, and `test_all` runs it second, after `test_quick_factor` and before `test_lucas_lehmer`, through `run_in_order`.

The planned test will use Theorem 2: stage 1 with base 3 and the exponent $E_1 = 2p \prod r^{e_r}$, a left-to-right powering modulo $M_p$, then the gcd; and stage 2, first prime by prime as above and later by one of the faster methods cited here. It will report a factor only after an exact re-check that it divides $M_p$, and treat a gcd equal to $M_p$ as no result.

## Provenance

Theorems 1 and 2 are proved in full above from Fermat's little theorem and the form of factors. The method is [Pollard1974](references.md#pollard1974), as [MontgomerySilverman1990, the abstract](references.md#montgomerysilverman1990) records and [CrandallPomerance2005, § 5.4](references.md#crandallpomerance2005) presents it, and its adaptation to Mersenne numbers is the one [GIMPSMath](references.md#gimpsmath) documents. The faster stage 2s are [Montgomery1987](references.md#montgomery1987), [MontgomerySilverman1990](references.md#montgomerysilverman1990) and [MontgomeryKruppa2008](references.md#montgomerykruppa2008). The planned test will be built from these papers and documentation, and from nothing drawn from another program.
